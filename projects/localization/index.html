Localization

Know where the robot is, know when it does not, and get it back

259 s to 60 s whole-site search, same benchmark

What was wrong

GPS is excellent outdoors and not to be trusted under a roof. LiDAR is excellent near structure and drifts in the open. A site robot crosses between the two all day.

The first version treated this as a choice: pick whichever sensor looks better right now. It worked until the two disagreed, and then the robot jumped. When it lost track completely, nothing could tell it where to look, and somebody had to click a position on a map. A robot that needs a person to tell it where it is cannot work alone.

What the robot can do now

  • Find itself. Load a map and it works out where it is, with or without GPS. Nobody has to click a starting position.
  • Stay continuous. One estimator produces the pose, so two sensors disagreeing no longer shows up as a robot that jumps.
  • Drive through outages. When GPS or the LiDAR match drops out for a while, the robot keeps going carefully on what it still has, and knows how much to trust itself.
  • Admit it is lost. If it truly loses its place it stops, says so and tries to find itself again. When it does, it carries on with the job.
  • Handle awkward machines. The same estimator runs on robots whose body does not point where their wheels do.

How

The estimator is a factor graph over LiDAR scan matching, RTK positions, wheel odometry and inertial data. Finding the robot from nothing uses global registration on geometric features, and no candidate is believed without an independent check. How satellite positions and the LiDAR map are brought into one frame is the subject of the first patent application, so I will leave it at that.

The six centimetres

On one robot the pose was about 6 cm RMS off against RTK, and tuning the estimator was not moving it. The estimator was not the problem. The inputs disagreed about time: when a sensor said it had measured was a little different from when it really had. Fixing when, not what, took the error to 3.9 cm RMS. It is the least glamorous and most useful thing on this page.

How I know

  • 3.9 cm RMS against RTK-fixed positions, on recorded drives from a robot in the fleet.
  • With three kinds of fault injected into replayed drives, no pose the robot published was more than 0.5 m off.
  • Searching a whole site for the robot went from 259 s to 60 s on the same map and the same computer.
  • Replaying a recorded drive with long outages, the time without a trusted pose fell by half, and what was left was on ground that is not in the map at all.
